Rectangle splitter

Split an X × Y rectangle of squares (each 3 × 3 cells, never cut through) into n rectangles of similar area so that the total length of shared edges, and with it the data the pieces must exchange, is as small as possible.

How it works

The big rectangle is X × Y squares. Each square is 3 × 3 cells and may not be cut through, so every cut runs along square boundaries. Lengths are shown in squares; one square edge is 3 cells.

Shared edges are the total length of all boundaries between two different pieces. The outer border doesn't count. Every inner edge belongs to exactly two pieces, which gives the key identity:

shared edges = Σ (wᵢ + hᵢ) − X − Y

So the goal is to make the pieces' half-perimeters as small as possible, which means pieces as close to square as possible. It also gives a lower bound: a piece of area a needs at least min(w + h) over all w × h = a that fit. Combining the cheapest allowed areas that add up to X·Y gives a value no layout can beat. A small knapsack-style DP computes it in O(n² · Δ²), where Δ is the number of allowed piece areas.

Similar size: every piece must have an area within ±T % of the average X·Y / n. The two whole numbers nearest the average are always allowed. If two layouts have the same shared-edge length, the one whose areas are closer to the average wins (smaller sum of squared deviations).

Data exchange

Pieces that share an edge must swap data. Along every square edge of a shared boundary, the middle cell on each side is sent across: R is needed by the square to its right and L by the square to its left. Vertically it is D (needed by the square below) and U (needed by the square above). Edges on the outer border of the big rectangle exchange nothing, and neither do squares inside the same piece.

So each shared square edge costs 2 exchanges (one cell each way) of B bytes each (4 by default): exchanges = 2 × shared edges and data = exchanges × B. Fewer shared edges therefore always means fewer exchanges, and the layout with the fewest exchanges is marked “best”. The pieces table shows how many cells each piece sends; it receives the same number.

Algorithms

Running times use these symbols: X, Y = width and height in squares; n = number of pieces; K = how many different piece counts one region can take within the tolerance (1 at ±0 %, about 1 + 2·T·n in general, never more than n); S = number of allowed piece shapes w × h; P = number of sub-rectangle sizes that fit exactly 5 pieces; d(n) = number of divisors of n.
